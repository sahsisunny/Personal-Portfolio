## When I Joined Real Dev Squad: What I Built & What I Learned

Real Dev Squad (RDS) is a community-driven open-source developer ecosystem that models how high-performing, distributed engineering organizations operate. There is no hand-holding: contributors build, review, and maintain production-grade web applications, developer triage portals, and real-time backend microservices under strict peer review and automated testing.

My experience at Real Dev Squad wasn't just another project—it was the crucible that shaped my engineering discipline, taught me how distributed teams build at scale, and took me on a multi-stack journey from Vanilla JavaScript to Ember.js, React, Next.js, and Golang.

---

### 1. When I Joined: The 5:00 AM Standup Ritual & Engineering Culture

When I joined Real Dev Squad in December 2022, the first thing that set it apart from any other community was its relentless engineering discipline. 

Every single morning, seven days a week, the squad gathered for a mandatory **5:00 AM IST daily standup call**.

Waking up before dawn every day to discuss code was intense, but it transformed my relationship with software engineering:

- **Strict Accountability:** Every contributor had to clearly report three things: what pull requests or tickets they merged yesterday, what they were shipping today, and what technical blockers were in their way.
- **RFC (Request for Comments) Culture:** If you wanted to modify a database schema, introduce a new API route, or refactor a shared UI component, you could not simply open a PR. You had to draft an RFC detailing the motivation, interface contracts, backward compatibility, and security considerations, and defend it during morning calls.
- **Uncompromising Peer Reviews:** Every pull request required multiple peer approvals, 100% passing automated test suites, clean linting, and staging environment verification before touching the main branch.

The 5:00 AM standup routine built an enduring habit of daily focus, proactive communication, and radical transparency that I carry into every software team I work with today.

### 2. The Gatekeeper Challenge: The Vanilla JS Lift Simulation

In Real Dev Squad, nobody is handed real production issues or permitted to touch production repositories on day one. Regardless of prior experience, every aspiring contributor must first complete and pass the **Lift Simulation** challenge ([sahsisunny/Lift-Simulation](https://github.com/sahsisunny/Lift-Simulation)).

The reason was deliberate: before using modern frameworks like React, Next.js, or Ember, you had to prove you understood the core fundamentals of web development—pure Vanilla JavaScript, the DOM API, asynchronous event loops, and clean architectural separation—without relying on external npm packages or UI libraries.

#### The Architectural Requirements & Milestones

The task required building an interactive web application where users could configure dynamic numbers of floors and elevators, with the lifts responding intelligently to floor calls. The challenge was split into two strict milestones:

1. **Milestone 1 — Architecture Separation:**
   - **Central Data Store:** A single source of truth tracking all elevator positions, operational states (idle, moving up, moving down), door states, and pending floor call queues.
   - **JS Engine (Controller):** An algorithmic scheduler responsible for calculating which lift should be dispatched to an incoming floor call, optimizing for minimum passenger wait times and preventing elevator starvation.
   - **Dumb UI:** A presentation layer completely decoupled from business logic—it simply subscribed to state changes emitted by the controller and updated the DOM accordingly.

2. **Milestone 2 — Real-World Mechanics & Physical Timing:**
   - Elevator doors had to take exactly 2.5 seconds to open and 2.5 seconds to close.
   - Elevators had to travel at a realistic speed of 2.0 seconds per floor.
   - If an elevator was already traveling past a floor that requested a stop in the same direction, it had to stop and service that floor before continuing its journey.
   - The UI had to be fully responsive, supporting mobile viewports and touch interaction without layout glitches or animation jitter.

Only after deploying the application to Netlify, passing multi-round code reviews, and defending the algorithmic edge cases during morning standups did I earn the "Verified Contributor" status to begin taking on real production tasks across Real Dev Squad repositories.

### 3. Earning Official Membership: The 5-Vote Peer Endorsement

Contributing to repositories was only the first milestone. Within Real Dev Squad, there is a distinct distinction between an active contributor and an official **Member**.

To become an official Member with elevated privileges, you had to undergo a formal community voting process:

- **The 5-Vote Threshold:** A contributor had to secure a minimum of **5 endorsement votes** from existing core members who already held elevated privileges.
- **Elevated Privileges:** Once voted in, members received official invitations to the Real Dev Squad GitHub organization. This unlocked write and triage access, the ability to assign and manage issues, authority to review and approve community pull requests, and moderation privileges across squad communication channels.
- **Trust & Leadership:** Votes were not given lightly. Existing members evaluated whether you consistently attended 5:00 AM standups, wrote clean and tested code, supported newer contributors, and demonstrated long-term commitment to the community's standards.

Earning those 5 votes and being inducted into the GitHub organization was one of the proudest moments of my journey—it marked the transition from an aspiring learner to a trusted community steward with direct responsibilities over shared infrastructure.

### 4. What I Built: Progressing Across the Tech Stack

Once onboarded as a member, I contributed across multiple distributed repositories, deliberately stepping outside framework comfort zones to solve real production needs across frontend and backend services.

#### Phase 1: Vanilla JavaScript & HTML/CSS (`website-www`)

My initial production tasks focused on the squad's public-facing landing portal (`website-www`), written in lightweight, dependency-free vanilla JavaScript:

- **Join Flow Redirection:** Refactored the interactive onboarding join section, fixing broken redirection links and routing prospective members cleanly to Discord and GitHub onboarding flows.
- **Cross-Browser Asset Consistency:** Resolved navbar icon links and SVG rendering issues across different viewport scales without introducing heavy external icon libraries.

#### Phase 2: Ember.js & Glimmer Components (`website-my`)

The member portal (`website-my`) was built with **Ember.js** (Ember Octane, Glimmer components, tracked properties, and Mirage/QUnit testing). Working in Ember required learning its opinionated conventions and strict unidirectional data flow:

- **Out Of Office (OOO) Workflow (PR #569):** Built the complete UI flow allowing members to request temporary "Out Of Office" leave so sprint leads wouldn't assign critical sprint deliverables to inactive members.
- **Feature Flag Gating:** Wrapped the new OOO interface behind developer feature flags (`dev FF`), ensuring the experimental UI could be safely tested in staging by core reviewers before general availability.
- **Member Status Sync (PR #2001):** Integrated the client with backend data models so approved OOO requests would automatically update user future status badges across the community status board.

#### Phase 3: React & Next.js (`website-status` & `website-dashboard`)

On the roadmap and sprint triage portals, I transitioned into modern React and Next.js codebases:

- **Mock Service Worker (MSW) Custom Server Setup (`website-status`):** Resolved failing API mocks in local development by introducing a custom local SSL proxy server configuration, unblocking dozens of contributors whose local environments were failing MSW interceptors.
- **Task Pagination & "Load More" Controls (PR #698):** Engineered dynamic pagination controls with loading indicators on the community task board, preventing hundreds of completed tickets from flooding the DOM on initial render.
- **Intersection Observer Infinite Scrolling (`website-dashboard`, PR #711):** Built an automated infinite-scroll hook using the browser's native `IntersectionObserver` to seamlessly stream task and member activity logs without manual page refreshes.
- **Loading State Stability (Issue #724):** Diagnosed and resolved race conditions in request card components where rapid state updates caused layout shifts and stuck loading spinners.

#### Phase 4: Golang Microservices (`tiny-site-backend`) & Node.js Controllers

Real Dev Squad also maintained dedicated microservices in **Golang** alongside Node.js and Firestore backends:

- **Golang URL List Retrieval Route (`tiny-site-backend`, PR #66):** Developed an authenticated HTTP route in Go for the squad's custom URL shortener service (`tiny-site`). Handled user authentication tokens, structured JSON error responses, and wrote automated unit tests for the Go handler.
- **Task Creation Migration Middleware (`website-backend`, PR #2053, PR #1870, PR #1883):** Authored Node.js/Express controllers and routing middleware migrating legacy task creation requests to the centralized `/request` API endpoint, standardizing validation across services.
- **Discord Bot Slash Commands (`discord-slash-commands`, PR #220):** Added filters to parse online active contributors in the `#tracking-updates` Discord channel, helping squad leaders quickly identify available peers during morning standups.

### 6. What I Learned: The Core Takeaways

Reflecting on my time at Real Dev Squad, the technical skills I acquired were immense, but the mindset shifts were even more transformative:

#### 1. Daily Discipline Compounds Faster Than Talent
Waking up at 5:00 AM every morning for standup created an ironclad work ethic. Showing up consistently, having work ready to present, and openly discussing blockers taught me that consistency and daily progress beat occasional bursts of late-night motivation.

#### 2. Master Fundamentals, Not Frameworks
Building the Lift Simulation in pure Vanilla JS before touching React or Ember was one of the most valuable lessons of my career. When you understand DOM mutation lifecycles, event delegation, closures, and the browser event loop, learning a new framework—whether Ember Octane, Next.js App Router, or a Golang HTTP server—is just learning syntax, not magic.

#### 3. Separation of Concerns is Universal
The pattern enforced by the Lift Simulation (separating the Data Store, the Engine Controller, and the Dumb View) is the exact same architectural principle that powers production frontends in React (Redux/Zustand + custom hooks + presentational components) and backend microservices (database layer + service logic + HTTP handler). Clean architecture is universal across all languages.

#### 4. High-Trust Collaboration & Community Responsibility
Earning 5 votes from peers to become an official member taught me that engineering is fundamentally a social discipline. Code quality matters, but empathy, reliable communication, thorough documentation, and helping others grow are what truly define a great engineer.

Real Dev Squad was where I evolved from a developer who simply writes code into a disciplined software engineer who solves complex problems, respects distributed architecture, and thrives in high-trust engineering teams.
